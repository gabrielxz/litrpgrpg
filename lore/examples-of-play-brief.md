# Gradebreaker: Examples of Play Brief

Rewrite the existing examples of play and add examples where they help readers learn the game. Use the latest manuscript, tutorial, and eventual companion app specification as the sources of truth. Earlier drafts and illustrative dialogue may be outdated. Do not change a rule to make an example work; flag any ambiguity.

**Write the examples of play last.** The companion app has not been scoped or built, and its behavior will affect how the table plays. Do not invent its interface, prompts, capabilities, or division of labor with the GM. Once its design is settled, show actual interactions with it wherever they naturally matter: what a player or Gabriel does, what the app returns, what information is visible to whom, and how that changes a decision. The app should feel like part of the same table, not an advertisement or a separate demonstration. Examples that teach play without the app must still accurately represent that mode if the final rules support one.

## The organizing idea

Make the examples episodes in one continuing campaign. The players pursue survival, power, discovery, and one another's trust. Their choices accumulate. Later, the System offers futures based on patterns the reader has already witnessed. The desired reaction is: **“Oh. It noticed that.”**

Each substantial example should teach a procedure accurately *and* change what someone at the table understands about a character, relationship, or the world. Brief examples that clarify arithmetic do not need a dramatic revelation. Every episode must also make sense to a reader who opens the book at that chapter.

## Gabriel and the recurring players

Name the example GM **Gabriel**. He enjoys ingenious plans, presents risks and costs plainly, and is willing to say when a player found an option he had not considered. He can revise his interpretation of a character's action. His occasional dry humor and affectionate exchanges should emerge from particular situations; his investment in the players should be clear. The obstacles provide opposition. Gabriel need not perform antagonism.

Use a small, consistent group of players with distinct interests. Include a LitRPG reader who is new to tabletop roleplaying, but give that player insight and initiative beyond asking rules questions. Let players want things their characters' repeated choices complicate: a combat specialist who keeps protecting strangers, a cautious planner who cannot leave a mystery alone, or an optimizer who becomes attached to someone inconvenient. These are possible tensions, not fixed comic roles. Let everyone surprise us sometimes.

Give the cast concrete concerns that persist across examples: a person they helped, equipment they keep repairing, a resource they argued over, an obligation carried from ordinary life. Such details should affect decisions rather than decorate dialogue. Let sincere moments stand without mandatory jokes.

## The System and the hidden layer

Keep the System's voice impersonal. People supply the humor. It measures what happened, but its inferences about motives and patterns depend on evidence. Recognition may be accurate, incomplete, or occasionally mistaken. Do not make every offer a misclassification or every choice a moral diagnosis. Build important recognition from several significant moments so readers can form their own interpretation before the System announces its own.

Use brief *behind-the-screen* asides selectively to expose judgments a reader would otherwise miss: what Gabriel knows, what remains undecided, why he discloses a cost before a choice, how he handles an unforeseen plan, or why one action is not yet evidence of a stable trait. Preserve uncertainty where the players should experience it. An aside describes Gabriel's thinking; it is not an HVE note written during play.

Show the Session-End Sweep only at an actual session boundary. Gabriel recalls the three moments that stuck with him after play; he does not log each decision as it happens. Demonstrate selectivity: a spectacular kill may earn no tally while a quiet decision does. A later class, title, or other recognition should make its earlier evidence legible without pretending the System reads minds.

## Dialogue and instruction

Start with a situation and a meaningful choice, not an explanation of the rule. Let a player state an intention, give the rule when the table needs it, and show the roll, modifiers, comparison, and consequence clearly enough to teach. Players can misunderstand a cost and revise a decision once it is explained. Dialogue should sound like people thinking together rather than delivering polished speeches, generic adventure quips, or perfect aphorisms.

Insight scenes should welcome an awkward or plainspoken answer. Discovering a Principle must not feel like guessing Gabriel's preferred sentence. When an example includes app interaction, keep the human choices and rule consequences intelligible; the app should perform only what its finished specification says it can do.

Displayed arithmetic, resource costs, turn timing, System messages, and downstream character changes must agree with the final rules. Randomly generated dice are unnecessary; coherent and correct outcomes are essential.

## Three connected pilot episodes

Use these three episodes to establish the cast and the voice once the rules and companion app design are ready:

1. **A fight** with a consequential defensive or resource choice. Show Gabriel making a fair ruling and a player doing something revealing under pressure.
2. **An ordinary disagreement over a scarce resource.** Make the practical concerns credible; let the players discover a solution Gabriel had not planned for.
3. **A later recognition or class offer** that draws on both scenes and at least one other meaningful choice. The reader should recognize the evidence, even if the player disagrees with the System's interpretation.

Each pilot must teach its immediate rules and stand on its own. Together they should test whether the continuing story makes readers want to play with this table while giving them enough clarity to run their own.
